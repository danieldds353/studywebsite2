Explained answer key

References are printed textbook pages; add 25 for the supplied PDF viewer. Slide references identify the chapter-specific lecture PDF.

1. C. Determine Budget

Determine Budget aggregates and time-phases estimates to establish the cost baseline. Estimating produces the amounts and their basis; controlling compares performance and manages changes.

Chapter 7 · Process outputs · Printed pp. 288-289, 305-306 · Lecture slides 7-8, 26-28

2. B. The cost management plan

The plan sets control thresholds, performance measurement rules, units, organizational links, and reporting procedures. The baseline supplies approved time-phased budget values rather than the rules for managing them.

Chapter 7 · Cost management plan · Printed pp. 294 · Lecture slides 15

3. B. 10%

Profit is $300,000. Profit margin = profit / revenue = 300,000 / 3,000,000 = 10%. Dividing by expenditures gives a different ratio; dollars alone are not a margin.

Chapter 7 · Profit margin · Printed pp. 290 · Lecture slides 9

4. A. System B, by $40,000

A costs 80,000 + 4(40,000) = $240,000. B costs 120,000 + 4(20,000) = $200,000. Life cycle costing includes support, so choosing only the lowest deployment price would be misleading.

Chapter 7 · Life cycle costing · Printed pp. 290-291 · Lecture slides 9

5. A. Server: direct cost; shared electricity: indirect cost

Direct costs are attributable to the specific project. Shared overhead is normally allocated as indirect cost. These classifications are different from whether a cost is tangible or already sunk.

Chapter 7 · Cost classifications · Printed pp. 292 · Lecture slides 12

6. A. Do not continue merely to recover the $240,000; future costs exceed future benefits.

The $240,000 cannot be recovered by choosing to continue. Compare incremental future benefits of $60,000 with incremental future costs of $100,000; continuation loses another $40,000 under the stated assumptions.

Chapter 7 · Sunk cost decisions · Printed pp. 292-293 · Lecture slides 12

7. B. Baseline $200,000; total budget $215,000

The cost baseline includes contingency reserve: 180,000 + 20,000 = $200,000. Add management reserve outside that baseline to obtain the $215,000 total project budget.

Chapter 7 · Reserves and baseline · Printed pp. 293, 305-306 · Lecture slides 13, 26-27

8. A. $100,000 to $400,000

This edition gives ROM accuracy as -50% to +100%: 200,000(0.50) to 200,000(2.00). The tighter -5%/+10% and -10%/+25% ranges describe definitive and budgetary estimates.

Chapter 7 · Estimate accuracy · Printed pp. 295-296 · Lecture slides 17-18

9. A. Definitive

The textbook associates definitive estimates with accurate purchasing decisions and later planning. The slide table repeats a budget-planning purpose in that row; the textbook table and prose provide the more precise distinction.

Chapter 7 · Estimate purpose · Printed pp. 295-296 · Lecture slides 17

10. D. Analogous estimating

Using a similar past project as the reference is analogous/top-down estimating. A rate multiplied by a measurable driver is parametric; summing detailed work-item estimates is bottom-up.

Chapter 7 · Analogous versus parametric · Printed pp. 297-298 · Lecture slides 20

11. C. $105,000

The variable portion is 750 x $120 = $90,000. Add the separate setup cost once: $105,000. A parametric result depends on the driver and the rate being applicable to the actual work.

Chapter 7 · Parametric calculation · Printed pp. 298 · Lecture slides 20

12. D. It can improve detail and accuracy but takes more estimating effort.

Bottom-up estimating uses detailed work items and knowledgeable estimators. The extra detail can improve accuracy but is time-consuming; an incomplete WBS still produces an incomplete estimate.

Chapter 7 · Bottom-up estimates · Printed pp. 297-299 · Lecture slides 20-21

13. C. $60,000

Using the stated weighting, (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (40,000 + 220,000 + 100,000) / 6 = $60,000. A simple triangular average would be $65,000, but that is not the weighting requested.

Chapter 7 · Three-point cost estimating · Printed pp. 297 · Lecture slides 20

14. B. Its basis can be reviewed and revised when project conditions change.

A basis of estimates explains how an estimate was built and what it depends on. It improves review and updating but does not remove uncertainty or authorize changes. Surveyor Pro illustrates this documentation.

Chapter 7 · Basis of estimates · Printed pp. 299-304 · Lecture slides 22-24

15. D. 1 FTE and $3,200

Total project effort is 40 person-hours, equal to one full-time equivalent for a 40-hour week. Labor cost is 40 x $80 = $3,200. FTE measures effort relative to a full-time period, not headcount.

Chapter 7 · FTE and labor costing · Printed pp. 296-297, 305 · Lecture slides 19, 26

16. D. PV $20,000; EV $16,000

PV = 50% x 40,000 = $20,000. EV = 40% x 40,000 = $16,000. Actual spending is AC, not earned value; EV uses budgeted rates for the work achieved.

Chapter 7 · Planned value and earned value · Printed pp. 307-308 · Lecture slides 29-31

17. B. CV = -$6,000; SV = -$12,000

CV = EV - AC = 48,000 - 54,000 = -$6,000. SV = EV - PV = 48,000 - 60,000 = -$12,000. Both signs are unfavorable; SV is in budget-value units, not days.

Chapter 7 · EVM variance calculations · Printed pp. 308-309 · Lecture slides 30-32

18. C. Cost-efficient for work performed, but behind the planned work schedule

CPI above 1 means earned budget value exceeds actual cost for the work performed. SPI below 1 means earned work is below the value scheduled by this date. The two indexes measure different dimensions.

Chapter 7 · EVM performance interpretation · Printed pp. 309 · Lecture slides 31-32

19. C. EAC $300,000; ETC $180,000

CPI = 96,000 / 120,000 = 0.80. EAC = BAC / CPI = 240,000 / 0.80 = $300,000. ETC = EAC - AC = 300,000 - 120,000 = $180,000, the forecast cost remaining.

Chapter 7 · EAC and ETC · Printed pp. 308-310 · Lecture slides 31-34

20. D. 1.20

TCPI(BAC) = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC) = 120,000 / 100,000 = 1.20. The remaining work must earn $1.20 of budget value per $1 spent; current CPI is only 0.80.

Chapter 7 · TCPI target · Printed pp. 309 · Lecture slides 31-34

21. D. 12.5 months

The textbook shortcut divides original total duration by SPI: 10 / 0.80 = 12.5 months. This is a conditional estimate of total duration, not remaining time or a replacement for critical-path analysis.

Chapter 7 · Schedule forecasting limits · Printed pp. 308-310 · Lecture slides 33-34

22. A. Compare EV with AC and investigate unfinished work, rather than judge only AC versus PV.

Spending less can simply mean accomplishing less. Cost performance compares earned value with actual cost. Baseline changes require authorization; editing it to hide a variance destroys the comparison.

Chapter 7 · Cost control and changes · Printed pp. 306-311 · Lecture slides 28-34

23. B. Compare investments together and reprioritize or consolidate overlapping work.

Portfolio management evaluates the suite of investments together, considering duplication, strategic value, risk, and scarce funding. EVM helps assess delivery performance but does not by itself decide which investments should exist.

Chapter 7 · Project portfolio management · Printed pp. 312-315 · Lecture slides 35-38

24. C. Use high-level forecasts, detail near-term work, and reprioritize scope within funding constraints.

Adaptive estimating uses lightweight forecasts and just-in-time detail. With strict budgets, teams may adjust scope and schedule, prioritize value, and obtain funding by release; agile does not mean unlimited or untracked spending.

Chapter 7 · Adaptive cost management · Printed pp. 315-316 · Lecture slides 39

25. B. False

Goodwill is a typical intangible benefit because reliable monetary valuation is difficult. Intangible does not mean worthless; it describes difficulty in measurement.

Chapter 7 · Cost and benefit classifications · Printed pp. 292 · Lecture slides 12

26. A. True

Repetition can improve efficiency in a regular pattern. This is not a promise that every unrelated or novel task will become cheaper.

Chapter 7 · Learning curve theory · Printed pp. 293 · Lecture slides 13

27. B. False

Contingency reserve belongs in the cost baseline. Management reserve is outside the baseline and is added to form the total project budget.

Chapter 7 · Reserves · Printed pp. 293, 305-306 · Lecture slides 13, 26-27

28. A. True

EV may exceed time-to-date PV when work is ahead of plan. For a defined work package, EV does not exceed its total authorized budget merely because actual spending is high.

Chapter 7 · Earned value bounds · Printed pp. 307-310 · Lecture slides 29-34

29. A. True

When all planned scope is earned, EV equals total PV/BAC, so conventional SPI becomes 1. Its late-project behavior is a limitation; inspect actual dates and the schedule. This follows directly from the assigned EV/PV formula.

Chapter 7 · EVM completion limitation · Printed pp. 308-310 · Lecture slides 31-34

30. B. False

Software calculates from the inputs supplied. Weak estimates, inconsistent completion rules, and stale actuals can make precise-looking dashboards misleading.

Chapter 7 · Software and information quality · Printed pp. 310-311, 314-315 · Lecture slides 36-38

31. A. A gap in fitness for use despite conformance to the written requirements

Conformance addresses written specifications; fitness for use addresses whether the product serves its intended purpose. Stakeholder needs can be inadequately captured even when the build follows the specification.

Chapter 8 · Conformance and fitness for use · Printed pp. 330-331 · Lecture slides 6-7

32. B. Plan Quality Management

Planning identifies quality requirements, standards, metrics, and how they will be met. Manage Quality puts quality activities into operation; Control Quality measures actual results.

Chapter 8 · Quality process mapping · Printed pp. 330-332 · Lecture slides 7-10

33. D. A quality audit supporting Manage Quality

An audit reviews quality management activities and process effectiveness, often producing lessons and improvement opportunities. It differs from testing a specific deliverable for defects or acceptance.

Chapter 8 · Quality audits · Printed pp. 334-335 · Lecture slides 11

34. B. Control Quality produces a verified deliverable; Validate Scope addresses formal customer acceptance.

Figure 8-1 lists verified deliverables under Control Quality. Formal acceptance belongs to Validate Scope, covered in Chapter 5. Page 336 uses looser acceptance/validated wording; do not collapse the two process responsibilities.

Chapter 8 · Quality and scope handoff · Printed pp. 331-332, 336 · Lecture slides 7-8, 13

35. D. At least 95% of searches finish within 2 seconds with 500 concurrent users under the agreed test dataset.

A usable metric states what is measured, the threshold, and operating conditions. “Fast” alone leaves the test load, response threshold, and success proportion unspecified.

Chapter 8 · Operational quality metrics · Printed pp. 331-334 · Lecture slides 9-10

36. B. Reliability: operating as expected under normal conditions; maintainability: ease of upkeep and repair

Reliability concerns expected operation; maintainability concerns how readily the product can be maintained. Both influence quality but describe different properties.

Chapter 8 · Reliability versus maintainability · Printed pp. 333-334 · Lecture slides 10

37. C. A fishbone/cause-and-effect diagram

A fishbone organizes possible causes of an observed problem. It supports investigation and the five-whys technique; listing a possible cause does not prove it.

Chapter 8 · Cause-and-effect analysis · Printed pp. 336-337 · Lecture slides 14-15

38. C. 75%

There are 100 complaints total. Login plus timeout = 50 + 25 = 75, or 75%. Pareto analysis identifies the largest contributors; the 80/20 guideline is not an arithmetic rule forcing every dataset to equal 80%.

Chapter 8 · Pareto interpretation · Printed pp. 340-341 · Lecture slides 14, 20

39. D. Investigate a possible nonrandom pattern even though the observations meet specifications.

The assigned rule flags seven successive points on one side of the mean or increasing/decreasing. Statistical stability and customer conformance are separate; a signal calls for investigation, not an automatic defect count.

Chapter 8 · Control versus specification limits · Printed pp. 337-338 · Lecture slides 14, 16

40. A. A checksheet/tally sheet

A checksheet structures collection of occurrences. Its data may later support a histogram or Pareto chart, but the immediate job is reliable tallying.

Chapter 8 · Checksheet selection · Printed pp. 339 · Lecture slides 14, 17

41. B. The variables appear associated; further analysis is needed to establish causation.

Scatter plots reveal possible relationships between two variables. A pattern alone does not rule out confounding variables or prove a causal mechanism.

Chapter 8 · Scatter diagrams · Printed pp. 339 · Lecture slides 14, 18

42. C. A flowchart

Flowcharts show process steps, decisions, and paths. Run charts show measurements in time order; they answer a different question about variation and trends.

Chapter 8 · Run charts and flowcharts · Printed pp. 341-342 · Lecture slides 14, 21-22

43. A. 385

n = 0.25(1.96 / 0.05)^2 = 384.16; the minimum whole-number sample is 385. The textbook displays 384 as an approximate example. The question explicitly requests rounding up.

Chapter 8 · Statistical sample calculation · Printed pp. 342-343 · Lecture slides 23-24

44. A. It becomes four times as large.

Error appears in the denominator and is squared. Replacing e with e/2 multiplies (z/e)^2 by four. Confidence level and margin of error are separate design choices.

Chapter 8 · Confidence and precision · Printed pp. 343 · Lecture slides 23-24

45. C. Analyze

Analyze investigates and verifies root causes using evidence. Improve develops and pilots solutions; Control sustains and checks the resulting improvements.

47. C. 2,000

Total opportunities = 2,000 x 5 = 10,000. DPMO = 20 / 10,000 x 1,000,000 = 2,000. Count defects against all defined opportunities, not only against the number of forms.

48. C. Integration testing

Integration testing checks grouped components and their interactions. Unit testing examines components individually; system testing examines the full system, and UAT assesses fit with users’ business needs.

50. A. Training: prevention; retesting: appraisal

Training aims to prevent defects. Retesting evaluates the corrected output and is appraisal. The separate work to fix a pre-delivery defect is internal failure; the question asks specifically about training and retesting.

External failure means the defect escaped to the customer. Correcting it before delivery is internal failure; prevention and inspection are conformance-related activities.
